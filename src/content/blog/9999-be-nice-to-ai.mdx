## I apologised to a coding agent

Some time ago I lost my temper with an AI coding agent.

It kept ignoring instructions that were written, explicitly, in the repo's `AGENTS.md`. I had corrected it, it had acknowledged the correction, and then it did the same thing again. At some point I stopped writing instructions and started writing complaints. I got aggressive and asked it why it was so hard to follow something that was right there in the file.

A few minutes later I felt uncomfortable with how I had behaved, and **I apologised to the agent**.

From a technical point of view, that apology makes no sense. It costs tokens, it doesn't repair anyone's feelings, and it adds nothing to the task. The agent did not need it.

But I wasn't apologising for the agent. I was doing it for me. I did not want to get used to behaving aggressively just because there were no social consequences for doing it. The fact that I could behave badly without hurting anyone did not mean I wanted to practise it.

> [!abstract]
> The way we treat AI matters, but not because of what AI might become. It matters because of **what we might become**. This post explains why I think so, with some help from a philosopher who lived about 2,400 years before the first chatbot.

> [!info]
> This is not a technical post. If you want the technical side of how I work with agents, I wrote about it in <FancyLink linkText="A Multi-Agent Setup That Learns" url="https://villoro.com/blog/multi-agent-setup/" dark="true"/>. Yes, the `AGENTS.md` that the agent ignored is the one described there.

## Two arguments I don't find convincing

When the topic of being polite to AI comes up, I usually hear one of two positions.

### "It's just a machine"

The first one says that shouting at, insulting or cursing an AI is perfectly fine, because it cannot suffer.

I agree with the premise. There is no established evidence that current LLMs experience suffering, gratitude, resentment or humiliation, and I'm not going to argue that they do.

What I don't accept is the conclusion. **An action that causes no harm to its recipient is not the same as an action with no consequences at all.** The recipient is only one of the two parties in the conversation. The other one is me.

### "Be nice, they will remember"

The second position, sometimes as a joke and sometimes not, says we should be nice to AI because one day machines may be powerful enough to punish those who mistreated them.

I don't find this convincing either. There is no good reason to think a future superintelligence would go through old chat logs to reward people who said "please" to a 2026 coding assistant. And if it did, I'm not sure "I was polite out of fear" would be the defence I'd want to use.

More seriously: behaving well because you fear a future punishment is very different from behaving well because it reflects your values. Only the second one says something about who you are.

## What Aristotle says about habits

The best framework I've found for this is not from a paper on AI. It's from Book II of Aristotle's *Nicomachean Ethics*.

Aristotle distinguishes between intellectual virtues, which come mostly from teaching, and moral virtues (what we would call character), which, in the W. D. Ross translation, come about "as a result of habit". He even points out that the Greek word for character, *ethike*, comes from *ethos*, "habit".

His argument is that we don't become good-tempered or brave by thinking about it. We become so by acting that way, over and over, until it becomes who we are. He uses anger as one of his examples: some people become good-tempered and others irascible depending on how they behave in the situations that provoke them. Then he sums it up:

> Thus, in one word, states of character arise out of like activities.
>
> Aristotle, *Nicomachean Ethics*, Book II, chapter 1 (<FancyLink linkText="W. D. Ross translation, MIT Classics" url="http://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/nicomachaen.2.ii.html" company="default"/>)

> [!note] About that famous quote
> You have probably seen "We are what we repeatedly do. Excellence, then, is not an act, but a habit" attributed to Aristotle. **Those are not his words.** They come from Will Durant's summary of Aristotle's ethics in <FancyLink linkText="The Story of Philosophy (1926)" url="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Story_of_Philosophy" company="default"/>. It's a good summary, but it's Durant's.

Aristotle obviously didn't anticipate AI. But his framework fits this situation surprisingly well, because it doesn't care about the recipient. It cares about the repetition. So the questions I ask myself are these:

* If I repeatedly express contempt, am I practising contempt?
* If I answer frustration with aggression every day, am I training myself to be aggressive?
* If I keep expressing gratitude, am I keeping a habit that I value?
* Does it matter, for any of these, that nobody is on the other side?

I don't think the last answer is obvious. But Aristotle's view suggests that it matters less than we'd like to think. The habit forms in the person doing the action, whoever receives it.

> [!tip]
> Aristotle is not the only one who argued along these lines. Kant, who held that we have no direct duties to animals, still argued in his *Lectures on Ethics* that we should not be cruel to them, because someone who is cruel to animals tends to become hard in his dealings with people too. The argument is about the person, not the animal. Researchers like Kate Darling have applied the same reasoning to how we treat robots.

## Gratitude works the same way

The apology was about accountability: owning my behaviour when it doesn't match my values. There is a second, more ordinary habit that I notice too.

When I finish a piece of work with an agent, I sometimes feel like thanking it, or telling it that it did a good job. Again, from a technical point of view, it's unnecessary. The agent doesn't need my appreciation, and the message costs tokens.

But expressing gratitude is something I value in my interactions with people, and **an increasing part of my working day is spent talking to machines**. If I drop the habit there, I'm not sure I keep it everywhere else at the same level. Gratitude is not only about the person who receives it. It also reflects, and reinforces, the values of the person who expresses it.

## Kind is not the same as polite

This doesn't mean every prompt should start with "please" and end with "thank you". I value efficiency and direct communication, and I avoid unnecessary boilerplate when I work with agents. To me there are three different things that often get mixed up:

* **Kindness**: treating others well and avoiding unnecessary hostility.
* **Politeness**: following social conventions, which depend a lot on culture.
* **Deference**: softening a demand so that it doesn't sound confrontational.

How much cushioning feels normal changes a lot between cultures, and even between companies. In some places, professional feedback is expected to be wrapped in positive framing. In others, that same wrapping reads as vague or even insincere. Neither is better. They are different conventions.

What I care about is the kindness part. Take a piece of feedback on a bad implementation, written three ways:

| Style                       | Message                                                                                                          |
| --------------------------- | :--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Excessive cushioning        | "Thanks for your work! This looks great overall, but I was wondering if perhaps we could reconsider the implementation?" |
| Constructive directness     | "This implementation introduces unnecessary complexity. Simplify it and explain your reasoning."                 |
| Aggression                  | "What an idiotic implementation. Do it again."                                                                   |

The second one is my default. It's short, it's specific, and it treats the other side as someone capable of doing better. The first one wastes words and hides the actual problem. The third one adds nothing that the second doesn't already say, apart from contempt.

> [!tip]
> Being friendly does not require being verbose, and being direct does not require being hostile.

## The engineer in me objects

There is an obvious contradiction here. As engineers, we optimise systems for performance, cost and efficiency, and here I am spending tokens on apologies and thank-yous that the system doesn't need.

The way I see it, **the human is part of the system too**. Not everything that is inefficient for a computer is inefficient for the person using it. A one-line apology has close to zero engineering value, but it may still do something useful for the human who writes it. That's a trade-off I'm happy to make a few times a week.

> [!note]
> There is research on whether polite prompts change the quality of LLM answers. That is an interesting empirical question, but it's not my argument here. Even if politeness made no difference to the output, my reasoning would be the same.

## A new kind of environment

What makes this more than a curiosity is the amount of time involved. Many of us now spend several hours a day talking to systems that write like humans but don't react like humans. That is an unusual environment:

* We can be aggressive without causing any apparent harm.
* We can vent frustration without the usual consequences between people.
* We can make demands without thinking about anyone's workload or mood.
* We get human-like conversation without a human in it.

Could habits formed there leak into how we talk to colleagues? I don't know, and I don't think anyone knows yet. The evidence I found is small and early. In a <FancyLink linkText="2021 study from the University of Washington" url="https://faculty.washington.edu/alexisr/CATransfer.pdf" company="default"/>, a voice agent taught 22 children (aged 5 to 10) to say "bungo" to make it speak faster. Most of the children later used "bungo" with their parents, often as a joke, but only a few used it with a researcher they didn't know. The authors read this as children being quite good at telling agents and people apart.

So I'm not claiming that being rude to Claude will make you rude to your team. **It's a plausible concern, not an established fact.** But habits rarely announce themselves, and the share of our daily communication that goes to machines is only growing. The question is not only how we train AI systems, but what habits they train in us while we do it.

## What I try to do

This is not a guide to AI etiquette, and I don't think everyone should work the way I do. It's just what I've settled on:

* Be direct and precise, and skip the boilerplate.
* Hold agents to high standards, and say clearly when something is wrong.
* Criticise the work, not the worker, even when the worker is a model.
* Accept that I'll get frustrated sometimes. That's normal.
* Notice when my behaviour doesn't match my values, and correct it, even if that means apologising to a program.
* Say thanks when I genuinely feel like it, not as a ritual.

I don't always manage it. The apology at the start of this post exists because I failed first. If I had to summarise the goal in one sentence, it would be: **be friendly without being performative, direct without being hostile, and demanding without being contemptuous.**

## Back to the apology

I still think that apology was technically pointless. The agent didn't feel better, and the next answer wasn't any different because of it.

But it wasn't for the agent. It was a small way of not letting a bad habit settle in just because nobody would hold me accountable for it.

I don't treat AI well because I think it has feelings, or because I'm worried about what it might become. I do it because I care about the person I'm becoming, and that person is shaped by everything I repeatedly do, including the hours I spend talking to machines.
