## Motivation

Every data team has a **hero**: the person on rotation who deals with production failures. When an ECS job or a Prefect flow breaks, the hero reads the raw stack trace, guesses the likely cause, decides what to do, and — more often than not — reruns the job by hand.

It works, but it is slow, repetitive, and it depends entirely on who is on call that week. The same OOM error gets diagnosed from scratch every time. Context about *which owner to ping* or *whether this job is safe to rerun* lives in people's heads.

This post is the first in a series about a project I've been building to change that: a **failure triage service** — internally, the "DE hero agent" — that takes on the manual toil of the hero, and is designed to take on *more* of it over time.

> [!note]
> This is a vision post. It describes where the project is going and the decisions that shape it. Later posts in the series cover the actual implementation as each milestone ships.

## The problem with alerts today

Right now, each job sends its own Slack alert directly from the job code. That has three consequences:

- **Alert logic is duplicated** across every ECS job and Prefect flow.
- **Alerts are raw**: a stack trace dumped into a channel, with no summary, no classification, and no clear next step.
- **Nothing is stored**. Once the hero fixes the problem, all the context — what broke, why, what they did — is gone. There is no history to learn from.

The last point is the important one. If we ever want to automate any of this, we need a record of what failures look like and how humans respond to them. Alerts-as-Slack-messages give us none of that.

## The core idea: a centralized triage service

Instead of each job shouting into Slack, jobs emit a **structured failure event** to a single service. That service owns everything that happens next:

| Responsibility        | What it does                                                         |
| --------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Store**             | Persist every failure (and later, every human action) as a record.  |
| **Enrich & classify** | Turn a raw error into a summary and a likely cause.                  |
| **Alert**             | Post one clean, consistent Slack message with the right owner ping.  |
| **Interact**          | Let the hero act from the Slack thread — and record what they chose. |

Jobs get dumber; the service gets smarter. All the failure-handling logic lives in one place, and — crucially — every failure and every decision is now written down.

## Direction: increasing autonomy in stages

The whole project is built around one idea: **it should gain autonomy gradually, and only where we can prove it's safe.** I think of it as three stages.

### Stage 1 — Report

The service receives structured failure events, stores them, summarizes and classifies them, and posts a clear Slack alert. No actions yet — it just makes failures *easier to understand*.

### Stage 2 — Act on human decision

The hero interacts with the Slack thread: rerun the job, ask the owner, mark it as manually fixed, mark it as solved. The service performs the action **only when a human asks**, and it records every decision as an audit trail.

### Stage 3 — Act autonomously

Once the audit trail shows which actions are *consistently* safe — "this class of failure is always fixed by a rerun" — the service starts proposing those actions, and eventually performing them on its own, escalating to a human only when it's unsure.

> [!info]
> Only stages 1 and 2 are in scope right now. But every decision is made with stage 3 in mind. That's why we store state and human actions from day one: **the audit trail is the evidence base that lets us safely promote automation later.**

## The decisions that make this work

A few design choices are what keep this from becoming a scary "AI runs my production pipelines" project.

### The model advises, it never acts

This is the single most important rule. We use an LLM (via AWS Bedrock) for what LLMs are good at: **summarizing** a messy error and **classifying** the likely cause. That output is a *suggestion*, shown to the hero.

Whether an action is actually allowed — can this job be rerun? — is decided by **deterministic, application-owned code**, never by the model.

> [!warning]
> Letting a model decide to rerun production jobs is exactly the kind of thing that erodes trust fast. Keeping the model on the "advise" side of a hard boundary is what makes the rest of the roadmap defensible.

This boundary is also what makes stage 3 possible. Because the policy is code, we can look at the audit trail, add a rule, and know *exactly* what will and won't happen.

### It must fail safe

The service sits **in the production alerting path**. If it's down, or the database is unreachable, or the AI call times out — failures still need to reach a human.

So the guiding rule is: **degrade, never swallow**. If AI enrichment fails, fall back to a plain alert. If storage fails, still send the alert. A failure event should never disappear just because part of the triage stack had a bad day.

### Store everything now, automate later

We persist two things: **failure events** (what broke) and, later, **actions** (what the hero decided). This is deliberately more than stage 1 needs. It's the training/evidence base for stage 3 — and it's why the data is relational and queryable rather than a pile of Slack messages.

## Why not just...?

A few alternatives I considered and set aside:

- **Keep alerts in each job** — no history, no central logic, no path to automation.
- **A pure rule-based classifier** — brittle, and misses the "summarize this ugly trace" win that LLMs are genuinely good at.
- **Let an AI agent framework own the whole workflow** — hands the model control over production actions, which is exactly the line we don't want to cross yet.
- **Analyze Slack messages after the fact** — makes Slack the source of truth, which it should never be.

The through-line: keep orchestration and policy in our own code, use AI only where it clearly helps, and never give up auditability.
